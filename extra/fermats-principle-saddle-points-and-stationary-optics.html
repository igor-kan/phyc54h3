Fermat’s Principle Beyond Least Time: Stationary Paths, Saddle Points, and Gravitational Lensing

A rigorous mathematical exploration of the second variation of optical path length, conjugate points, caustic phase shifts, and saddle-point images in astrophysics.

NoteHistorical and Conceptual Context

In introductory physics, Fermat’s Principle is almost universally christened the “Principle of Least Time”. While historically understandable—Pierre de Fermat formulated the hypothesis in 1662 to deduce Snell’s Law by asserting that nature always operates via the most economical path—the nomenclature is physically and mathematically misleading.

The true physical law, rooted in the stationary phase approximation of the path integral, asserts that the optical path length is stationary (\(\delta S = 0\)), which admits not only local minima, but also local maxima and saddle points (minimax extrema).

1. Mathematical Formulation of Fermat’s Principle

Let physical 3-space be modeled as a Riemannian manifold \((\mathcal{M}, g)\) with coordinate chart \(\mathbf{r}(u) = (x^1(u), x^2(u), x^3(u))\) parameterized by an arbitrary monotonic curve parameter \(u \in [u_1, u_2]\) connecting fixed endpoints \(A = \mathbf{r}(u_1)\) and \(B = \mathbf{r}(u_2)\).

Let \(n(\mathbf{r})\) be the scalar refractive index of the isotropic, non-dispersive medium. The infinitesimal arc length is: \[ ds = \sqrt{g_{ij}(\mathbf{r}) \frac{dx^i}{du} \frac{dx^j}{du}} \, du = |\mathbf{\dot{r}}(u)| \, du \]

The total travel time functional \(T[\mathbf{r}]\) along a virtual path \(\gamma: A \to B\) is given by: \[ T[\mathbf{r}] = \int_{\gamma} dt = \int_{\gamma} \frac{ds}{v(\mathbf{r})} = \frac{1}{c} \int_{u_1}^{u_2} n(\mathbf{r}(u)) |\mathbf{\dot{r}}(u)| \, du = \frac{1}{c} S[\mathbf{r}] \] where \(S[\mathbf{r}] = \int n(\mathbf{r}) ds\) is the Optical Path Length (OPL) functional, and \(c\) is the speed of light in vacuum.

2. Explicit Physical and Mathematical Assumptions

To analyze the character of the stationary points with mathematical rigor, we state our baseline assumptions:

  1. Geometric Optics Approximation (\(\lambda \to 0\) / Eikonal Limit): The wavelength of electromagnetic radiation \(\lambda\) is negligible compared to the characteristic scale of spatial variations in the refractive index: \[ \frac{|\nabla n|}{n} \ll \frac{1}{\lambda} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{|\nabla^2 n|}{|\nabla n|} \ll \frac{1}{\lambda} \]
  2. Isotropy and Non-Dispersive Medium: \(n = n(\mathbf{r})\) is a positive scalar function depending purely on position, independent of ray propagation direction \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\) or temporal frequency \(\omega\).
  3. Smoothness and Differentiability: The boundary surfaces and the index profile \(n(\mathbf{r})\) are at least \(C^2\)-differentiable, ensuring that first and second functional variations are well-defined.
  4. Boundary Constraint: Endpoints \(A\) and \(B\) are held strictly fixed under all virtual variations (\(\delta \mathbf{r}(A) = \delta \mathbf{r}(B) = \mathbf{0}\)).
  5. Static Metric (in Gravitational Contexts): When generalizing to curved spacetime, the background gravitational field admits a timelike Killing vector field \(\partial_t\), allowing an absolute time coordinate to parameterize null geodesics.

3. First and Second Variations: The Geometry of Extrema

To classify whether a stationary ray \(\mathbf{r}_0(u)\) minimizes, maximizes, or creates a saddle point in travel time, we perform a Taylor expansion of the functional \(S[\mathbf{r}_0 + \epsilon \boldsymbol{\eta}]\) in powers of the variation parameter \(\epsilon\), where \(\boldsymbol{\eta}(u_1) = \boldsymbol{\eta}(u_2) = \mathbf{0}\): \[ S[\mathbf{r}_0 + \epsilon \boldsymbol{\eta}] = S[\mathbf{r}_0] + \epsilon \, \delta S[\mathbf{r}_0; \boldsymbol{\eta}] + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2} \, \delta^2 S[\mathbf{r}_0; \boldsymbol{\eta}] + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^3) \]

The First Variation (\(\delta S = 0\))

Choosing the parameter \(u\) to be the arc length along the unperturbed ray \(s \in [0, L]\), the Lagrangian is \(L(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') = n(\mathbf{r}) \sqrt{\mathbf{r}' \cdot \mathbf{r}'} = n(\mathbf{r})\), where \(\mathbf{r}' = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds}\) is the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf{\hat{t}}\) (\(|\mathbf{r}'| = 1\)).

Applying the Euler-Lagrange equations: \[ \frac{d}{ds} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}'} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{0} \implies \frac{d}{ds} \left( n(\mathbf{r}) \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds} \right) = \nabla n(\mathbf{r}) \] This is the canonical Ray Equation of Geometric Optics. Any path satisfying this differential equation is a stationary point of \(S[\mathbf{r}]\).

The Second Variation (\(\delta^2 S\)) and the Jacobi Equation

The classification of the stationary point is governed entirely by the sign of the second variation: \[ \delta^2 S[\mathbf{r}_0; \boldsymbol{\eta}] = \int_0^L \left[ P_{ij}(s) \frac{d\eta^i}{ds} \frac{d\eta^j}{ds} + Q_{ij}(s) \eta^i \eta^j \right] ds \] where \(\boldsymbol{\eta}(s)\) represents a normal perturbation vector orthogonal to the ray trajectory (\(\boldsymbol{\eta} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = 0\)).

  1. Local Minimum (\(\delta^2 S > 0\) for all \(\boldsymbol{\eta} \neq \mathbf{0}\)): Every adjacent virtual path takes strictly longer than the true ray.
  2. Local Maximum (\(\delta^2 S < 0\) for all \(\boldsymbol{\eta} \neq \mathbf{0}\)): Every adjacent virtual path takes strictly less time than the true ray. (Note: Over arbitrary variations without boundary constraints, an absolute maximum does not exist because a path can be zig-zagged infinitely to yield \(S \to \infty\). A local maximum occurs only when restricted to a finite-dimensional constraint manifold, such as reflection off a curved surface).
  3. Saddle Point / Minimax (\(\delta^2 S > 0\) for some variations, \(\delta^2 S < 0\) for others): Perturbing the path along one normal coordinate direction increases travel time, while perturbing along an orthogonal normal coordinate decreases travel time.

4. Physical Case 1: Reflection Off Curved Mirrors (The Ellipsoidal Criterion)

The most intuitive realization of stationary points that are neither maxima nor minima occurs in catoptrics (reflection off curved surfaces).

Consider light traveling in a homogeneous medium (\(n = 1\)) from source \(A\) to receiver \(B\) via a single specular reflection at point \(P\) on a reflecting boundary surface \(\Sigma\):

The Reference Ellipsoid

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be a prolate spheroid (ellipsoid of revolution) with focal points at \(A\) and \(B\). By definition of an ellipse: \[ \text{For any point } Q \in \mathcal{E}: \quad |\mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_Q| + |\mathbf{r}_Q - \mathbf{r}_B| = L_0 = \text{constant} \]

Let a real physical mirror \(\Sigma\) be tangent to the ellipsoid \(\mathcal{E}\) at point \(P\). The law of reflection (\(\theta_i = \theta_r\)) ensures that the normal vector to \(\Sigma\) at \(P\) coincides with the normal to \(\mathcal{E}\) at \(P\). Therefore, \(P\) is rigorously a stationary point (\(\delta T = 0\)).

Quantitative Mathematical Proof of a Reflection Saddle Point

Let the reflection point be \(P = (0, 0, 0)\) in Cartesian coordinates. Let the mirror surface \(\Sigma\) be locally represented as a quadratic Monge patch: \[ z(x, y) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \kappa_x x^2 + \kappa_y y^2 \right) + \mathcal{O}(x^3, y^3) \] where \(\kappa_x\) and \(\kappa_y\) are the principal curvatures of the mirror at the origin.

Let source \(A = (-d \sin\theta, 0, d \cos\theta)\) and receiver \(B = (d \sin\theta, 0, d \cos\theta)\) lie symmetrically in the \(xz\)-plane at distance \(d\) and angle of incidence \(\theta\).

The optical path length for reflection at an arbitrary adjacent point \(Q = (x, y, z(x, y))\) on the mirror is: \[ D(x, y) = \sqrt{(x + d\sin\theta)^2 + y^2 + (z - d\cos\theta)^2} + \sqrt{(x - d\sin\theta)^2 + y^2 + (z - d\cos\theta)^2} \]

Expanding \(D(x, y)\) in a multi-variable Taylor series about \((x, y) = (0, 0)\): \[ D(x, y) = 2d + \left. \frac{\partial D}{\partial x} \right|_0 x + \left. \frac{\partial D}{\partial y} \right|_0 y + \frac{1}{2} \left[ D_{xx} x^2 + 2 D_{xy} xy + D_{yy} y^2 \right] + \dots \]

1. First Derivatives (Stationary Condition)

\[ \frac{\partial D}{\partial x}(0, 0) = \sin\theta - \sin\theta = 0, \quad \frac{\partial D}{\partial y}(0, 0) = 0 \] The first variation vanishes identically, confirming the specular law of reflection.

2. Second Derivatives (The Hessian Matrix)

Computing the second partial derivatives: \[ D_{xx} = \frac{2\cos^2\theta}{d} - 2\kappa_x \cos\theta = 2\cos\theta \left( \frac{\cos\theta}{d} - \kappa_x \right) \] \[ D_{yy} = \frac{2}{d} - 2\kappa_y \cos\theta = 2 \left( \frac{1}{d} - \kappa_y \cos\theta \right) \] \[ D_{xy} = 0 \]

The critical curvature thresholds for the reference ellipsoid are: \[ \kappa_{E, x} = \frac{\cos\theta}{d}, \quad \kappa_{E, y} = \frac{1}{d\cos\theta} \]

3. Classification via the Hessian Determinant

The Hessian matrix is diagonal: \[ \mathcal{H} = \begin{pmatrix} D_{xx} & 0 \\ 0 & D_{yy} \end{pmatrix} \implies \det(\mathcal{H}) = D_{xx} D_{yy} \]

  • Condition for a Saddle Point: A saddle point occurs when \(\det(\mathcal{H}) < 0\), which requires \(D_{xx}\) and \(D_{yy}\) to have opposite signs: \[ \left( \kappa_x - \frac{\cos\theta}{d} \right) \left( \kappa_y - \frac{1}{d\cos\theta} \right) < 0 \]

Physical Realization: Construct a mirror with a cylindrical or hyperbolic paraboloid curvature such that: \[ \kappa_x > \frac{\cos\theta}{d} \quad \text{and} \quad \kappa_y < \frac{1}{d\cos\theta} \] * Along the \(x\)-displacement axis: \(D_{xx} < 0\), meaning light traversing an adjacent point along the \(x\)-axis arrives faster than the specular ray (local maximum along \(x\)). * Along the \(y\)-displacement axis: \(D_{yy} > 0\), meaning light traversing an adjacent point along the \(y\)-axis arrives slower than the specular ray (local minimum along \(y\)). * Conclusion: The actual physical trajectory taken by the light ray is a rigorous saddle point.

5. Physical Case 2: Conjugate Points and Caustic Crossings

In refraction and focusing optics, stationary points transition from minima to saddles whenever a ray passes through a conjugate point (caustic focus).

Source A ──────────────[ Converging Lens ]────────────── (Focal Point C) ────────────── Receiver B
                                                               │
                             Before C: Strict Minimum          │  After C: SADDLE POINT
                             δ²S > 0                           │  Morse Index μ = 1

The Morse Index Theorem in Optics

Let \(\gamma\) be an unperturbed ray connecting \(A\) and \(B\). A point \(C \in \gamma\) is defined as conjugate to \(A\) if there exists a non-trivial Jacobi field \(\mathbf{J}(s)\) along \(\gamma\) that vanishes at both \(A\) and \(C\): \[ \mathbf{J}(A) = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{J}(C) = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{J} \not\equiv \mathbf{0} \]

According to the Morse Index Theorem: 1. If the trajectory from \(A\) to \(B\) contains no conjugate points, the second variation \(\delta^2 S\) is strictly positive-definite. The path is a local minimum. 2. For every conjugate point \(C\) crossed between \(A\) and \(B\), the quadratic form \(\delta^2 S\) acquires an additional negative eigenvalue. 3. Therefore, if light passes through a focus or caustic before reaching receiver \(B\), the travel time functional along the ray is no longer a minimum, but a saddle point of Morse index \(\mu = 1\) (or higher).

The Relativistic Fermat Principle

In curved spacetime \((\mathcal{M}, g_{\mu\nu})\), light travels along null geodesics (\(ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu} dx^\mu dx^\nu = 0\)). In a stationary, weak-field spacetime parameterized by the Newtonian gravitational potential \(\Phi(\mathbf{r}) \ll c^2\), the line element is: \[ ds^2 = -\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) c^2 dt^2 + \left(1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) (dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2) = 0 \]

Solving for the coordinate speed of light \(v = \frac{d\ell}{dt}\) yields an effective refractive index: \[ n_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{c}{v} \approx 1 - \frac{2\Phi(\mathbf{r})}{c^2} > 1 \] Because \(\Phi \le 0\), the presence of mass slows coordinate light speed, creating the gravitational Shapiro time delay.

The Arrival-Time Surface \(\tau(\boldsymbol{\theta})\)

In the thin-lens approximation, let: * \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be the true angular position of the source on the sky. * \(\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be the observed angular position in the lens plane. * \(D_L, D_S, D_{LS}\) be the angular diameter distances to the lens, to the source, and between lens and source.

The total light travel time (arrival time) functional \(\tau(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is: \[ \tau(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1 + z_L}{c} \frac{D_L D_S}{D_{LS}} \left[ \frac{1}{2} |\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}|^2 - \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right] \] where: * \(\frac{1}{2} |\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}|^2\) is the geometric time delay (deviation from a straight line). * \(\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{2}{c^2} \frac{D_{LS}}{D_L D_S} \int \Phi \, dz\) is the 2D effective gravitational deflection potential (the gravitational Shapiro delay).

Fermat’s principle dictates that observable images form where the gradient of arrival time vanishes: \[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \tau(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0} \implies \boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \] This reproduces the canonical Gravitational Lens Equation.

Image Classification via the Hessian of Arrival Time

The nature of each lensed image is classified by the Hessian matrix of the arrival time surface: \[ \mathcal{H}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial^2 \tau}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} \propto \delta_{ij} - \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} = \mathcal{A}_{ij}^{-1} \] where \(\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is the Jacobian magnification tensor of the gravitational lens.

The eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) of the inverse magnification tensor \(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\) categorize the images into three distinct topological types:

7. Wave Optics and Quantum Foundations: The Gouy Phase Shift

Why does nature select saddle points and maxima if light is classically supposed to take the “shortest” path?

The resolution is provided by Richard Feynman’s Path Integral Formulation of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED):

  1. A photon does not explore a single path; it explores all possible histories connecting \(A\) and \(B\).
  2. The total probability amplitude \(\Psi(B, A)\) is the functional sum over all virtual paths \(\gamma\): \[ \Psi(B, A) = \int \mathcal{D}[\mathbf{r}(t)] \, \exp\left( \frac{i}{\hbar} S_{\text{QED}} \right) = \int \mathcal{D}[\mathbf{r}(t)] \, \exp\left( i \omega T[\mathbf{r}] \right) \]
  3. In the macroscopic limit (\(\omega \to \infty\) or \(\lambda \to 0\)), neighboring paths with differing arrival times interfere destructively because their phase factors \(e^{i \omega T}\) oscillate rapidly: \[ \frac{d}{d\mathbf{r}} (\omega T) \neq 0 \implies \text{Phase cancellation} \to 0 \]
  4. Only where the first variation vanishes (\(\delta T = 0\)) do adjacent paths have identical phases to first order, producing coherent constructive interference.
  5. Constructive interference requires stationarity, not minimality. The wave does not care whether the stationary plateau is a bowl (minimum), an inverted dome (maximum), or a mountain pass (saddle).

### Historical and Conceptual Context
In introductory physics, **Fermat's Principle** is almost universally christened the *"Principle of Least Time"*. While historically understandable—Pierre de Fermat formulated the hypothesis in 1662 to deduce Snell's Law by asserting that nature always operates via the most economical path—the nomenclature is physically and mathematically misleading. 

The true physical law, rooted in the stationary phase approximation of the path integral, asserts that **the optical path length is stationary ($\delta S = 0$)**, which admits not only local minima, but also **local maxima** and **saddle points (minimax extrema)**.
:::

## 1. Mathematical Formulation of Fermat's Principle

Let physical 3-space be modeled as a Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M}, g)$ with coordinate chart $\mathbf{r}(u) = (x^1(u), x^2(u), x^3(u))$ parameterized by an arbitrary monotonic curve parameter $u \in [u_1, u_2]$ connecting fixed endpoints $A = \mathbf{r}(u_1)$ and $B = \mathbf{r}(u_2)$.

Let $n(\mathbf{r})$ be the scalar refractive index of the isotropic, non-dispersive medium. The infinitesimal arc length is:
$$
ds = \sqrt{g_{ij}(\mathbf{r}) \frac{dx^i}{du} \frac{dx^j}{du}} \, du = |\mathbf{\dot{r}}(u)| \, du
$$

The total travel time functional $T[\mathbf{r}]$ along a virtual path $\gamma: A \to B$ is given by:
$$
T[\mathbf{r}] = \int_{\gamma} dt = \int_{\gamma} \frac{ds}{v(\mathbf{r})} = \frac{1}{c} \int_{u_1}^{u_2} n(\mathbf{r}(u)) |\mathbf{\dot{r}}(u)| \, du = \frac{1}{c} S[\mathbf{r}]
$$
where $S[\mathbf{r}] = \int n(\mathbf{r}) ds$ is the **Optical Path Length (OPL)** functional, and $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   FERMAT'S VARIATIONAL PRINCIPLE                       │
│                                                                        │
│   The physical trajectory taken by a light ray connecting two fixed   │
│   points A and B is an extremal (stationary point) of the action:      │
│                                                                        │
│                 δS[r] = δ ∫ n(r(u)) |dr/du| du = 0                     │
│                                                                        │
│   subject to fixed boundary conditions: δr(u₁) = 0 and δr(u₂) = 0.     │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 2. Explicit Physical and Mathematical Assumptions

To analyze the character of the stationary points with mathematical rigor, we state our baseline assumptions:

1. **Geometric Optics Approximation ($\lambda \to 0$ / Eikonal Limit)**: The wavelength of electromagnetic radiation $\lambda$ is negligible compared to the characteristic scale of spatial variations in the refractive index:
   $$
   \frac{|\nabla n|}{n} \ll \frac{1}{\lambda} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{|\nabla^2 n|}{|\nabla n|} \ll \frac{1}{\lambda}
   $$
2. **Isotropy and Non-Dispersive Medium**: $n = n(\mathbf{r})$ is a positive scalar function depending purely on position, independent of ray propagation direction $\mathbf{\hat{k}}$ or temporal frequency $\omega$.
3. **Smoothness and Differentiability**: The boundary surfaces and the index profile $n(\mathbf{r})$ are at least $C^2$-differentiable, ensuring that first and second functional variations are well-defined.
4. **Boundary Constraint**: Endpoints $A$ and $B$ are held strictly fixed under all virtual variations ($\delta \mathbf{r}(A) = \delta \mathbf{r}(B) = \mathbf{0}$).
5. **Static Metric (in Gravitational Contexts)**: When generalizing to curved spacetime, the background gravitational field admits a timelike Killing vector field $\partial_t$, allowing an absolute time coordinate to parameterize null geodesics.

## 3. First and Second Variations: The Geometry of Extrema

To classify whether a stationary ray $\mathbf{r}_0(u)$ minimizes, maximizes, or creates a saddle point in travel time, we perform a Taylor expansion of the functional $S[\mathbf{r}_0 + \epsilon \boldsymbol{\eta}]$ in powers of the variation parameter $\epsilon$, where $\boldsymbol{\eta}(u_1) = \boldsymbol{\eta}(u_2) = \mathbf{0}$:
$$
S[\mathbf{r}_0 + \epsilon \boldsymbol{\eta}] = S[\mathbf{r}_0] + \epsilon \, \delta S[\mathbf{r}_0; \boldsymbol{\eta}] + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2} \, \delta^2 S[\mathbf{r}_0; \boldsymbol{\eta}] + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^3)
$$

### The First Variation ($\delta S = 0$)
Choosing the parameter $u$ to be the arc length along the unperturbed ray $s \in [0, L]$, the Lagrangian is $L(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}') = n(\mathbf{r}) \sqrt{\mathbf{r}' \cdot \mathbf{r}'} = n(\mathbf{r})$, where $\mathbf{r}' = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds}$ is the unit tangent vector $\mathbf{\hat{t}}$ ($|\mathbf{r}'| = 1$).

Applying the Euler-Lagrange equations:
$$
\frac{d}{ds} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}'} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{0} \implies \frac{d}{ds} \left( n(\mathbf{r}) \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds} \right) = \nabla n(\mathbf{r})
$$
This is the canonical **Ray Equation of Geometric Optics**. Any path satisfying this differential equation is a stationary point of $S[\mathbf{r}]$.

---

### The Second Variation ($\delta^2 S$) and the Jacobi Equation
The classification of the stationary point is governed entirely by the sign of the **second variation**:
$$
\delta^2 S[\mathbf{r}_0; \boldsymbol{\eta}] = \int_0^L \left[ P_{ij}(s) \frac{d\eta^i}{ds} \frac{d\eta^j}{ds} + Q_{ij}(s) \eta^i \eta^j \right] ds
$$
where $\boldsymbol{\eta}(s)$ represents a normal perturbation vector orthogonal to the ray trajectory ($\boldsymbol{\eta} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = 0$).

```
                      ┌───────────────────────────────────────┐
                      │    SPECTRUM OF SECOND VARIATION δ²S   │
                      └──────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                                         │
                 ┌───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
                 ▼                       ▼                       ▼
      ┌─────────────────────┐ ┌─────────────────────┐ ┌─────────────────────┐
      │   STRICT MINIMUM    │ │     SADDLE POINT    │ │   STRICT MAXIMUM    │
      │ δ²S[η] > 0 for all  │ │ δ²S[η₁] > 0 along u │ │ δ²S[η] < 0 for all  │
      │ non-zero variations │ │ δ²S[η₂] < 0 along v │ │ non-zero variations │
      └─────────────────────┘ └─────────────────────┘ └─────────────────────┘
```

1. **Local Minimum ($\delta^2 S > 0$ for all $\boldsymbol{\eta} \neq \mathbf{0}$)**: Every adjacent virtual path takes *strictly longer* than the true ray.
2. **Local Maximum ($\delta^2 S < 0$ for all $\boldsymbol{\eta} \neq \mathbf{0}$)**: Every adjacent virtual path takes *strictly less* time than the true ray. *(Note: Over arbitrary variations without boundary constraints, an absolute maximum does not exist because a path can be zig-zagged infinitely to yield $S \to \infty$. A local maximum occurs only when restricted to a finite-dimensional constraint manifold, such as reflection off a curved surface).*
3. **Saddle Point / Minimax ($\delta^2 S > 0$ for some variations, $\delta^2 S < 0$ for others)**: Perturbing the path along one normal coordinate direction increases travel time, while perturbing along an orthogonal normal coordinate decreases travel time.

## 4. Physical Case 1: Reflection Off Curved Mirrors (The Ellipsoidal Criterion)

The most intuitive realization of stationary points that are neither maxima nor minima occurs in catoptrics (reflection off curved surfaces).

Consider light traveling in a homogeneous medium ($n = 1$) from source $A$ to receiver $B$ via a single specular reflection at point $P$ on a reflecting boundary surface $\Sigma$:

```
                                  Source A
                                     \
                                      \
                                       \
                          Reflecting    \
                           Surface Σ     v
             ────────────────────────────P────────────────────────────
                                        /
                                       /
                                      v
                                  Receiver B
```

### The Reference Ellipsoid
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be a prolate spheroid (ellipsoid of revolution) with focal points at $A$ and $B$. By definition of an ellipse:
$$
\text{For any point } Q \in \mathcal{E}: \quad |\mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_Q| + |\mathbf{r}_Q - \mathbf{r}_B| = L_0 = \text{constant}
$$

Let a real physical mirror $\Sigma$ be tangent to the ellipsoid $\mathcal{E}$ at point $P$. The law of reflection ($\theta_i = \theta_r$) ensures that the normal vector to $\Sigma$ at $P$ coincides with the normal to $\mathcal{E}$ at $P$. Therefore, $P$ is rigorously a stationary point ($\delta T = 0$).

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     THE CURVATURE COMPARISON THEOREM                   │
│                                                                        │
│   Let κ_Σ be the principal curvatures of the mirror surface Σ at P,    │
│   and κ_E be the principal curvatures of the confocal ellipsoid E:     │
│                                                                        │
│   1. PLANE OR CONVEX MIRROR (κ_Σ < κ_E):                               │
│      The mirror Σ lies completely outside the ellipsoid E.             │
│      For any adjacent point Q ∈ Σ, OPL(Q) > L₀.                        │
│      ==> STRICT LOCAL MINIMUM.                                         │
│                                                                        │
│   2. HIGHLY CONCAVE SPHERICAL MIRROR (κ_Σ > κ_E in all directions):     │
│      The mirror Σ curves inside the ellipsoid E.                       │
│      For any adjacent point Q ∈ Σ, OPL(Q) < L₀.                        │
│      ==> STRICT LOCAL MAXIMUM.                                         │
│                                                                        │
│   3. ASTIGMATIC / SADDLE MIRROR (κ_Σ,₁ > κ_E,₁ and κ_Σ,₂ < κ_E,₂):     │
│      The mirror curves inside along axis 1, and outside along axis 2.  │
│      ==> SADDLE POINT (NEITHER MINIMUM NOR MAXIMUM).                   │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Quantitative Mathematical Proof of a Reflection Saddle Point

Let the reflection point be $P = (0, 0, 0)$ in Cartesian coordinates. Let the mirror surface $\Sigma$ be locally represented as a quadratic Monge patch:
$$
z(x, y) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \kappa_x x^2 + \kappa_y y^2 \right) + \mathcal{O}(x^3, y^3)
$$
where $\kappa_x$ and $\kappa_y$ are the principal curvatures of the mirror at the origin.

Let source $A = (-d \sin\theta, 0, d \cos\theta)$ and receiver $B = (d \sin\theta, 0, d \cos\theta)$ lie symmetrically in the $xz$-plane at distance $d$ and angle of incidence $\theta$.

The optical path length for reflection at an arbitrary adjacent point $Q = (x, y, z(x, y))$ on the mirror is:
$$
D(x, y) = \sqrt{(x + d\sin\theta)^2 + y^2 + (z - d\cos\theta)^2} + \sqrt{(x - d\sin\theta)^2 + y^2 + (z - d\cos\theta)^2}
$$

Expanding $D(x, y)$ in a multi-variable Taylor series about $(x, y) = (0, 0)$:
$$
D(x, y) = 2d + \left. \frac{\partial D}{\partial x} \right|_0 x + \left. \frac{\partial D}{\partial y} \right|_0 y + \frac{1}{2} \left[ D_{xx} x^2 + 2 D_{xy} xy + D_{yy} y^2 \right] + \dots
$$

#### 1. First Derivatives (Stationary Condition)
$$
\frac{\partial D}{\partial x}(0, 0) = \sin\theta - \sin\theta = 0, \quad \frac{\partial D}{\partial y}(0, 0) = 0
$$
The first variation vanishes identically, confirming the specular law of reflection.

#### 2. Second Derivatives (The Hessian Matrix)
Computing the second partial derivatives:
$$
D_{xx} = \frac{2\cos^2\theta}{d} - 2\kappa_x \cos\theta = 2\cos\theta \left( \frac{\cos\theta}{d} - \kappa_x \right)
$$
$$
D_{yy} = \frac{2}{d} - 2\kappa_y \cos\theta = 2 \left( \frac{1}{d} - \kappa_y \cos\theta \right)
$$
$$
D_{xy} = 0
$$

The critical curvature thresholds for the reference ellipsoid are:
$$
\kappa_{E, x} = \frac{\cos\theta}{d}, \quad \kappa_{E, y} = \frac{1}{d\cos\theta}
$$

#### 3. Classification via the Hessian Determinant
The Hessian matrix is diagonal:
$$
\mathcal{H} = \begin{pmatrix} D_{xx} & 0 \\ 0 & D_{yy} \end{pmatrix} \implies \det(\mathcal{H}) = D_{xx} D_{yy}
$$

* **Condition for a Saddle Point**: A saddle point occurs when $\det(\mathcal{H}) < 0$, which requires $D_{xx}$ and $D_{yy}$ to have **opposite signs**:
  $$
  \left( \kappa_x - \frac{\cos\theta}{d} \right) \left( \kappa_y - \frac{1}{d\cos\theta} \right) < 0
  $$

**Physical Realization**: Construct a mirror with a cylindrical or hyperbolic paraboloid curvature such that:
$$
\kappa_x > \frac{\cos\theta}{d} \quad \text{and} \quad \kappa_y < \frac{1}{d\cos\theta}
$$
* Along the $x$-displacement axis: $D_{xx} < 0$, meaning light traversing an adjacent point along the $x$-axis arrives **faster** than the specular ray (local maximum along $x$).
* Along the $y$-displacement axis: $D_{yy} > 0$, meaning light traversing an adjacent point along the $y$-axis arrives **slower** than the specular ray (local minimum along $y$).
* **Conclusion**: The actual physical trajectory taken by the light ray is a **rigorous saddle point**.

## 5. Physical Case 2: Conjugate Points and Caustic Crossings

In refraction and focusing optics, stationary points transition from minima to saddles whenever a ray passes through a **conjugate point (caustic focus)**.

```
Source A ──────────────[ Converging Lens ]────────────── (Focal Point C) ────────────── Receiver B
                                                               │
                             Before C: Strict Minimum          │  After C: SADDLE POINT
                             δ²S > 0                           │  Morse Index μ = 1
```

### The Morse Index Theorem in Optics
Let $\gamma$ be an unperturbed ray connecting $A$ and $B$. A point $C \in \gamma$ is defined as **conjugate** to $A$ if there exists a non-trivial Jacobi field $\mathbf{J}(s)$ along $\gamma$ that vanishes at both $A$ and $C$:
$$
\mathbf{J}(A) = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{J}(C) = \mathbf{0}, \quad \mathbf{J} \not\equiv \mathbf{0}
$$

According to the **Morse Index Theorem**:
1. If the trajectory from $A$ to $B$ contains **no conjugate points**, the second variation $\delta^2 S$ is strictly positive-definite. The path is a **local minimum**.
2. For every conjugate point $C$ crossed between $A$ and $B$, the quadratic form $\delta^2 S$ acquires an additional **negative eigenvalue**.
3. Therefore, if light passes through a focus or caustic before reaching receiver $B$, the travel time functional along the ray is **no longer a minimum**, but a **saddle point of Morse index $\mu = 1$ (or higher)**.

### The Relativistic Fermat Principle
In curved spacetime $(\mathcal{M}, g_{\mu\nu})$, light travels along null geodesics ($ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu} dx^\mu dx^\nu = 0$). In a stationary, weak-field spacetime parameterized by the Newtonian gravitational potential $\Phi(\mathbf{r}) \ll c^2$, the line element is:
$$
ds^2 = -\left(1 + \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) c^2 dt^2 + \left(1 - \frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right) (dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2) = 0
$$

Solving for the coordinate speed of light $v = \frac{d\ell}{dt}$ yields an effective refractive index:
$$
n_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{c}{v} \approx 1 - \frac{2\Phi(\mathbf{r})}{c^2} > 1
$$
Because $\Phi \le 0$, the presence of mass **slows coordinate light speed**, creating the gravitational Shapiro time delay.

### The Arrival-Time Surface $\tau(\boldsymbol{\theta})$

In the thin-lens approximation, let:
* $\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ be the true angular position of the source on the sky.
* $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ be the observed angular position in the lens plane.
* $D_L, D_S, D_{LS}$ be the angular diameter distances to the lens, to the source, and between lens and source.

The total light travel time (arrival time) functional $\tau(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is:
$$
\tau(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1 + z_L}{c} \frac{D_L D_S}{D_{LS}} \left[ \frac{1}{2} |\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}|^2 - \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right]
$$
where:
* $\frac{1}{2} |\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}|^2$ is the **geometric time delay** (deviation from a straight line).
* $\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{2}{c^2} \frac{D_{LS}}{D_L D_S} \int \Phi \, dz$ is the 2D **effective gravitational deflection potential** (the gravitational Shapiro delay).

Fermat's principle dictates that observable images form where the gradient of arrival time vanishes:
$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \tau(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0} \implies \boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\beta} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
$$
This reproduces the canonical **Gravitational Lens Equation**.

The nature of each lensed image is classified by the **Hessian matrix** of the arrival time surface:
$$
\mathcal{H}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial^2 \tau}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} \propto \delta_{ij} - \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} = \mathcal{A}_{ij}^{-1}
$$
where $\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the **Jacobian magnification tensor** of the gravitational lens.

The eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ of the inverse magnification tensor $\mathcal{A}^{-1}$ categorize the images into three distinct topological types:

## 7. Wave Optics and Quantum Foundations: The Gouy Phase Shift

Why does nature select saddle points and maxima if light is classically supposed to take the "shortest" path?

The resolution is provided by Richard Feynman's **Path Integral Formulation of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)**:

1. A photon does not explore a single path; it explores **all possible histories** connecting $A$ and $B$.
2. The total probability amplitude $\Psi(B, A)$ is the functional sum over all virtual paths $\gamma$:
   $$
   \Psi(B, A) = \int \mathcal{D}[\mathbf{r}(t)] \, \exp\left( \frac{i}{\hbar} S_{\text{QED}} \right) = \int \mathcal{D}[\mathbf{r}(t)] \, \exp\left( i \omega T[\mathbf{r}] \right)
   $$
3. In the macroscopic limit ($\omega \to \infty$ or $\lambda \to 0$), neighboring paths with differing arrival times interfere destructively because their phase factors $e^{i \omega T}$ oscillate rapidly:
   $$
   \frac{d}{d\mathbf{r}} (\omega T) \neq 0 \implies \text{Phase cancellation} \to 0
   $$
4. **Only where the first variation vanishes ($\delta T = 0$) do adjacent paths have identical phases to first order**, producing coherent constructive interference. 
5. Constructive interference requires **stationarity**, not minimality. The wave does not care whether the stationary plateau is a bowl (minimum), an inverted dome (maximum), or a mountain pass (saddle).
